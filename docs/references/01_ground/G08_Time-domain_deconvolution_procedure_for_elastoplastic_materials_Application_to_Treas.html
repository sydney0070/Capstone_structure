Time-domain deconvolution procedure for elastoplastic materials: Application to the Treasure Island site during the 1989 Loma Prieta earthquake

Abstract

When dynamic soil-structure interaction (DSSI) analyses are performed, e.g. using the finite element (FE) method, the input signal is required at the base of the model. Nevertheless, acceleration records are usually available at the surface and, therefore, the desired motion must be deconvolved to the base. The latter is usually performed through the solution of one-dimensional propagation of shear waves in an elastic medium, in the frequency domain. Herein, nonlinear behavior is generally incorporated through the equivalent-linear method, by iteratively reducing the stiffness and increasing the critical damping ratio as a function of the maximum strains attained in each iteration. However, if complex material models are adopted to characterize the soil, the input motion derived with the equivalent linear method will not be compatible due to the simplified approach used to represent the nonlinear behavior. In this article, the use of a procedure to perform a time-domain deconvolution in non-linear elastoplastic materials is demonstrated. The goal is to generate input accelerograms at the base of a FE model to perform DSSI analyses. The procedure is based on the iterative modification of the motion at the base according to the relative differences between the propagated and target surface spectra. To illustrate the use of the methodology, it was applied to a FE model of the Treasure Island site (San Francisco, US), to derive the required motion at the base from a record of the Loma Prieta earthquake.

Method details: iterative procedure

Earthquakes, such as those that occurred in Treasure Island in 1989, Mexico City in 1985, and 2017, among others, have illustrated the relevance of site effects on structural damage [1], [2], [3]. Even though epicentres occurred at considerable distances (80 km, 350 km, and 150 km, respectively), significant damage took place in urban areas due to the amplification of seismic waves caused by local site conditions. These observations confirmed the need of carrying out details studies and provide tools to improve the assessment of seismic risk, including phenomena that could increase the potential of damage [4], [5], [6], [7].

The capabilities of modern computers have increasingly allowed the implementation of more robust and complex methodologies, both for designing and the assessment of the expected performance of existing structures. Several studies have shown the importance and necessity of accounting for the interaction of the structure and the ground during an earthquake [8], [9], [10], [11], [12], especially in the presence of nonlinear soil deposits. The latter can be addressed through numerical time-domain dynamic soil-structure interaction (DSSI) analyses using, for example, the finite element (FE) method. In this approach, the soil can be characterized through complex nonlinear elastoplastic constitutive models, that can satisfactorily reproduce its cyclic behavior. However, the desired seismic motion is sometimes prescribed at the surface of the soil deposit, requiring its deconvolution to be applied at the base of the FE model. The latter is generally performed through one-dimensional frequency domain analysis, using the equivalent linear approach to account for the nonlinear behavior of the soil [1]. However, if a nonlinear elastoplastic constitutive model is adopted in the DSSI analysis, to characterize the soil behavior, the prescribed motion will not be recovered at the surface of the numerical model (in free field conditions) due to the different, and simplified, approach to the represent the soil's behavior during deconvolution.

In this article, a simple iterative procedure for time-domain deconvolution in non-linear elastoplastic materials [13] is applied. It represents a useful and necessary tool for its application in studies of DSSI, and it will help to face challenges related to a more realistic assessment and design of structures. The procedure is based on the modification of the motion at the base of the model according to the relative differences between the propagated and target surface acceleration spectra. Modifications of the motion at the base are performed in its spectrum, and the required acceleration time history is obtained in each iteration by means of a spectral matching technique. The derived input motion results in the desired surface spectrum when propagated through the FE model (in free-field conditions). The iterative deconvolution procedure is described below.

To start the procedure, the following initial elements must be defined: (a) a FE model of the site under study, (b) a surface target spectrum, SAtarget(T), and (c) an accelerogram that will be our initial input motion at the base of the model, ACCibase(t) (see Fig. 1), where T is the period of vibration and t is the time. All these elements will be defined by the user based on the particular needs of the case under study. Usually, the SAtarget(T) corresponds to a code design spectrum, a uniform hazard spectrum, or a response spectrum of a surface-recorded accelerogram. Regarding ACCibase(t), although the method can perform with any initial input motion, it is strongly recommended that the duration and frequency content of the adopted record represents adequately the seismic environment of the site to yield realistic results. In the case when the desired motion corresponds to a surface-recorded accelerogram, its frequency-domain deconvolution, with the equivalent linear method, is an excellent alternative to set the initial input motion ACCi=0base(t). This will also reduce the number of iterations required to reach convergence.

Let's start with the initial iteration (i=0). A FE site response analysis is performed by applying ACCibase(t) at the base to obtain the motion response at the surface, ACCisurface(t), and its response spectrum,SAisurface(T) (Fig. 1). Subsequently, a raw correction factor, Fi+1raw(T), is calculated from the relationship between the SAtarget(T) and the SAisurface(T) as follows:

Fi+1raw(T)=SAtarget(T)SAisurface(T) (1)

This factor represents the ratio between both spectra and is used to modify the response spectrum of ACCibase(t), called here SAibase(T). If Fi+1raw(T) is between 0.95 and 1.05, a value of 1.0 is assigned and, therefore no correction is performed in SAibase(T) for the specific period T considered. This range was arbitrarily defined to establish an acceptable tolerance, which also depends on the effectiveness of the spectral matching technique that will be used in the following steps. For further details please refer to [13].

Afterwards, an adjusted correction factor, Fi+1(T), is estimated to limit corrections and avoid abrupt variations in the response at the surface due to the specific amplification behavior at each period T. To this end, a transfer function or amplification factor, AFi(T), is calculated first as follows:

AFi(T)=SAisurface(T)SAibase(T) (2)

In this way, regions of the spectrum where relevant amplification occurs are identified. When Fi+1raw(T)  is applied without scaling in high amplification regions, the convergence of the analysis is compromised due to oscillations in the resulting surface spectral accelerations between iterations (more details in [13]). To compute the adjusted correction factor, Fi+1(T), the function, ai+1(T), is defined ranging from 0.5 for AFi(T)<1.2 and 0.15 for AFi(T)>2.0 (see Fig. 2). For AFi(T) values in-between 1.2 and 2.0, ai+1(T) is defined by the following linear relationship:

ai+1(T)=1.025−0.4375·AFi(T) (3)

Once the values of ai+1(T) have been determined, the new adjusted correction factor, Fi+1(T), is estimated as follows:

Fi+1(T)=1.0+(Fi+1raw(T)−1.0)ai+1(T) (4)

Here, the factor ai+1(T) is multiplied by the percentage change of Fi+1raw(T) to obtain Fi+1(T). This factor is applied to the SAibase(T) as follows:

SAi+1base(T)=SAibase(T)·Fi+1(T). (5)

As a result, a new response spectrum at the base of the model, SAi+1base(T), is obtained.

Now, to obtain the new input motion, ACCi+1base(t), a spectral matching technique is used to adjust ACCi=0base(t) assuming SAi+1base(T) as the target spectrum. The spectral matching method adopted accurately adjusts the Fourier amplitude spectrum of seed accelerograms to obtain compatible spectra with a given target. This matching technique has been used previously in [5,14]. More details on the spectral matching technique can be found in [13]. However, the user can adopt the spectral matching method that best suits his requirements, although results may vary depending on the method used.

A FE site response analysis is performed with ACCi+1base(t). As a result, a new surface motion, ACCi+1surface(t), and its response spectrum, SAi+1surface(T), are obtained. An error measure, E, is calculated between SAi+1surface(T) and SAtarget(T). This process is performed iteratively until the desired tolerance, TOL, is reached. A summary of the algorithm of the iterative procedure is shown in Fig. 3.

Method validation

The Loma Prieta earthquake of October 17, 1989, has been attributed to lateral and vertical movement of the San Andreas fault. The earthquake had a moment magnitude (Mw) of 7.0 and occurred at a depth of approximately 18 km. Around $125 million of economic losses were caused to the U.S. Navy facilities [15] as a result of this earthquake. The site of Treasure Island was formed by an artificial fill overlying soft Holocene Bay mud [16]. It is located in the San Francisco Bay, close to the Oakland Bay Bridge, and next to a large rocky outcrop called Yerba Buena Island. Throughout the Loma Prieta event, despite the proximity between these islands, significantly higher accelerations were recorded on Treasure Island (PGA90 = 0.16 g and PGA00 = 0.10 g) compared to those on Yerba Buena (PGA90 = 0.068 g and PGA00 = 0.031 g). This ground motion amplification is attributed to local site effects. Therefore, this case study was adopted for the application and validation of the described deconvolution procedure [13].

To this end, first, the initial elements are defined:

    FE model of the site under study

The Treasure Island seismic station at the Naval Base Fire Station, which belongs to the California Strong Motion Instrumentation Program, was selected as the site under study. Stratigraphic conditions were determined according to what was reported in Gibbs et al. [16] and Roy & Sharma [17], where shales, with shear wave velocities in excess of 700 m/s, were identified at a depth of about 88 m. For instance, Gibbs et al. [16] provide a soil profile with layers’ thicknesses and shear wave velocities. Roy & Sharma [17] summarizes the main properties and parameters of the soil at the site. A summary of the main soil parameters adopted is shown in Table 1.

The FE model was constructed and analysed using the PLAXIS 2D software [18]. Since only free-field conditions are considered, the FE model corresponds to a slender column of soil (unit width) with special lateral boundary conditions to account for the semi-infinite lateral extent of the deposit [19]. The ACCibase(t) is applied at the base of the model through a compliant base [20] that minimizes wave reflections. An elastoplastic constitutive model [21] was adopted to account for the nonlinear response of the soil. The model includes features like small-strain stiffness degradation, a hyperbolic hardening law, and a Mohr-Coulomb-type limit surface.

Parameters for the adopted model were calibrated to reproduce a realistic behavior of the materials (see Table 1). The stiffness degradation and damping ratio curves of the different materials were selected from the results of Hashash & Park [22], Seed et al. [23], and Lodde [24]. The calibration was carried out through a script in Python for Plaxis 2D. This code simulates a laboratory test (through the “Soil test” tool) in which a soil specimen is subjected to different levels of angular deformation. The correlations defined by Brinkgreve [25], Obrzud & Truty [26] were also considered in the calibration process.

  • (b)

    Surface target spectrum, SAtarget(T)

The response spectrum of the accelerogram corresponding to the East-West (90°) horizontal component recorded at the Naval Base Fire Station during the Loma Prieta earthquake was selected as SAtarget(T). This represents the maximum elastic response of different oscillators (T) with a critical damping of 5% during the Loma Prieta earthquake in Treasure Island. The selected SAtarget(T) is shown In Fig. 4.

  • (c)

    Initial input motion at the base of the model, ACC0base(t)

The E-W (90°) horizontal as-recorded component of the 1989 Loma Prieta earthquake (Fig. 5) was selected as ACC0base(t). This accelerogram represents the surface acceleration recorded in the Naval Base Fire Station in Treasure Island. The ground motion record was obtained from the PEER Ground Motion Database [27]. In this case, we could have selected the ground motion record in Yerba Buena (rocky outcrop) or used a deconvoluted signal from a traditional method (e.g. linear equivalent method) to obtain faster convergence. Herein, the surface acceleration records were used to demonstrate the effectiveness of the procedure regardless of the input signal used.

Upon establishing the initial elements, the process of deconvolution commences. In the initial iteration (i = 0), a site response analysis is performed with ACCi=0base(t) and the surface response ACCi=0surface(t) is obtained (step 4 of Fig. 3). A visual comparison of these signals is provided in Fig. 6a. When comparing the response spectrum of ACCi=0surface(t) with the SAtarget(T), it is observed that there is a relevant difference between both spectra (Fig. 6b). This was expected since the surface record was used as ACCi=0base(t). As this motion interacts with the underlying soil deposit, it incites amplification, consequently resulting in an increase in the spectral response.

The correction factors are computed to reduce the differences between these spectra (steps 5 and 6 of Fig. 3). These factors will allow us to modify the input signal until obtaining a surface spectrum equivalent to the target. The raw correction factor, Fi=1raw(T), is shown in Fig. 7a. As observed in the results, the values obtained are not in the range between 0.95 and 1.05. Therefore, it was not necessary to assign values of 1. Then, the adjusted correction factor, Fi=1(T), is estimated (Fig. 7b).

Now, a new base response spectrum is computed, SAi=1base(T), by multiplying Fi=1(T) with SAi=0base(T) (step 7 of Fig. 3). A comparison of SAi=0base(T) and SAi=1base(T) is shown in Fig. 8a. Please keep in mind that, due to the particular circumstances of this validation, the SAi=0base(T) spectrum is equal to SAtarget(T). The new input motion, ACCi=1base(t), is computed through a spectral matching technique (step 8 of Fig. 3). To do this, ACCi=0base(t) is used as seed signal to match SAi=1base(T). More details regarding the spectral matching technique are available in [13]. ACCi=1base(t) obtained after the matching is compared with ACCi=0base(t) in Fig. 8b. The differences between both signals can be identified at a glance.

The site response analysis is computed again, but now with ACCi=1base(t), and a new surface motion, ACCi=1surface(t), and its response spectrum, SAi=1surface(T), are determined (step 9 of Fig. 3). The mean squared error is estimated from the relationship between SAi=1surface(T) and SAtarget(T) (step 10 of Fig. 3). This process is performed iteratively until the desired tolerance (TOL) is reached. In this particular case, 30 iterations were performed to study the evolution of the iterative procedure. As seen in Fig. 9a, the error begins to stabilize from i = 17. After performing the 30 iterations, the lowest error was obtained for i = 29. In Fig. 9b, a comparison between the SAtarget(T) (black), the SAi=29surface(T) (red), and the response spectra corresponding to all iterations (light grey) are shown.

Fig. 10 displays the time histories of resulting motions corresponding to the optimal iteration (i = 29). A comparison between the initial input signal and the one obtained during the best iteration, denoted as ACCi=29base(t), reveals a reduction in strong motion amplitudes (Fig. 10a). This outcome was anticipated, as the initial iteration (i = 0) yielded a surface response spectrum with higher acceleration values than the desired spectrum (see Fig. 6b). Consequently, the input signal was progressively adjusted to converge to a surface spectrum resembling the target.

Furthermore, in Fig. 10b, the motion derived from the site response analysis during the optimal iteration is contrasted with the recorded motion at the Naval Base Fire Station in Treasure Island, CA (E-W component during the 1989 Loma Prieta event). Notably, there is a resemblance in amplitude between these signals in the strong motion segment. It's important to acknowledge that considerable amplifications were observed in the remaining part of the signal (excluding the strong segment). The potential influence on structural response resulting from the dissimilarity between these surface signals, despite their nearly identical response spectra, is a subject of interest and is presently undergoing investigation. This application example showcases the capability of the suggested methodology. Despite the fact that the initially chosen input motion generated a surface spectrum that differed significantly from the desired target, the iterative process effectively managed to calculate the appropriate input, achieving a commendable level of accuracy in aligning with the target surface response.

Conclusions

This paper presents the application of an iterative procedure for time domain deconvolution in inelastic materials. To illustrate the use of the methodology, it was applied to a FE site response analysis of the Treasure Island site (San Francisco, US) during the Loma Prieta earthquake. The objective of this approach is to generate input time histories for DSSI numerical analyses, where the target motion is specified at the surface of a soil deposit in terms of a response spectrum. Although the adopted initial input motion produced a surface spectrum quite different from the target, the iterative procedure was able to successfully derive the input motion required to match the surface target with reasonable accuracy. It is worth mentioning that this methodology has been employed with various input signals and different site conditions, consistently achieving satisfactory results [13].

Throughout the presented methodology, three elements play a significant role: the soil model (including the constitutive description and profile characterization), the starting input signal at the base, and the spectral matching technique. The greater the accuracy in each of these elements, the more consistent the signal produced at the surface will be. In this case study, we deliberately considered a nonideal starting motion to demonstrate the methodology's capabilities. We employed the signal recorded at the surface of the deposit during the Loma Prieta earthquake as the input signal at the base of the model. Typically, a signal recorded in a rocky outcrop or a deconvoluted signal obtained using a simplified technique (e.g., equivalent linear method) would be a much more suitable alternative. Starting from the first iteration (Fig. 6), we observe signal amplification, potentially resulting from resonance effects, leading to increased amplitudes across the signal. It is important to recall that the soil model employed represents actual site conditions, thus influencing the recorded/input signal and causing resonance amplifications. This effect is subsequently transferred to the signal at the base through the spectral matching technique. In the following iterations, we notice that the amplifications at the beginning and end of the signal continue to increase to achieve a surface signal that closely matches the desired response spectrum. This objective was successfully achieved. Nonetheless, these peaks observed at the beginning and end could be reduced by adopting more objective initial input signals, as the aforementioned alternatives.”
